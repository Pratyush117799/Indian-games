01 Identity & History

Pachisi is India's royal race game — a cross-and-circle board game played on a cross-shaped cloth board using cowrie shells as dice. Its name is derived from the Hindi word paccīs (पचीस), meaning twenty-five — the highest possible throw, the one that grants grace. That number became the game's identity.

The game's origins stretch back to at least the 4th century CE, with cruciform boards discovered in the Ellora Caves dated to the 6th–7th century. Similar dice-and-board combinations appear from the Painted Grey Ware period (Iron Age, 1100–800 BCE) at sites in Mathura and Noh. The Mahabharata references a dice game called "Pasha" — possibly an early ancestor — making this game's lineage one of the longest of any game still played today.

"The Court itself, divided into red and white squares, being the board, and an enormous stone raised on four feet, representing the central point... sixteen young slaves from the harem wearing the players' colours represented the pieces, and moved to the squares according to the throw of the dice."
— Louis Rousselet, on Emperor Akbar's Pachisi court at Fatehpur Sikri, 1876

The game's most famous moment in history: Emperor Akbar played Pachisi on a life-size court at Fatehpur Sikri, using human pieces — sixteen women from his harem, dressed in the four players' colours — moving across a vast red-and-white stone courtyard. The remains of this board are still visible at Fatehpur Sikri, Agra, and Allahabad.

Pachisi then spread across the world. Spanish traders took it home as Parchís. The British commercialised it as Parcheesi. In 1896, simplified versions became Ludo in England, and eventually Sorry! in America. Every family board night in modern India tracing back to Ludo — traces back to Pachisi.

02 Board Anatomy

The Pachisi board is cruciform — a symmetrical cross shape, traditionally embroidered on silk or velvet cloth in deep reds, crimsons, and gold thread. The cross shape represents the four directions of the cosmos; the centre represents the royal court — the beginning and end of all journeys.

Charkoni
चरकोनी · "The Royal Court"
The large central square. The starting and finishing point of all 16 pawns. All journeys begin here. Pieces must return here exactly to win — no overshooting. In Akbar's court, this was a raised stone dais.
The Four Arms
चार भुज · "Four Directions"
Each arm has 3 columns × 8 rows = 24 squares. Each player owns one arm. Pieces enter via their arm's centre column, then travel counter-clockwise around the outer track, and return up their own centre column to Charkoni.
Castle Squares
दुर्ग · "Fortress"
12 specially marked squares. A piece resting here cannot be captured. 4 are at the tip of each arm's centre column; 8 are 4 squares inward from the outer column ends. Key strategy: stack pawns on castles while you wait for a grace throw.
Home Column
गृह स्तम्भ · "Return Path"
The centre column of each arm is that player's private home stretch. Once a pawn re-enters its own arm's centre column, it cannot be captured. Pieces laid on their side signal they are "returning home."
03 Players & Teams

Pachisi is ideally played as a team game — two pairs of players, seated opposite each other. This is what makes it fundamentally different from Ludo: sacrificing your pawn to let your partner's advance is a valid and sophisticated strategy.

04 The Cowrie Shells — Dice of the Gods

Pachisi uses 6 cowrie shells (कौड़ी) as its dice — ancient currency, sacred object, and randomiser all in one. You throw them from your palm; the number that land with their opening (aperture) facing upward is your move value. The cowrie shell was the currency of ancient Indian trade — playing with them was quite literally gambling with money.

A Grace throw (6, 10, or 25) does two things: it allows you to introduce a new pawn onto the board from Charkoni, AND it grants you another turn. Grace throws are the heart of Pachisi's tension — you cannot enter any pawn without one, and getting three in a row is a legendary feeling.

05 Complete Rules
  1. Setup. All 4 pawns per player begin at the Charkoni (centre). Decide playing order by each player throwing — highest goes first. Play continues counter-clockwise.
  2. Entering the board. A pawn can only leave Charkoni on a Grace throw (6, 10, or 25). It enters on the first square of that player's arm's centre column. Throw again after a Grace.
  3. Movement path. From their arm's centre column, pawns move counter-clockwise around the outer track of the entire board, completing a full loop, and then re-enter their own arm's centre column heading home toward Charkoni.
  4. Castle squares (Safe zones). 12 squares marked as fortresses. A pawn resting here cannot be captured by any opponent. Multiple friendly pawns may share a castle. An occupied castle is closed to opponents.
  5. Capture. If a pawn lands on a square occupied by an opponent's pawn (not a castle), the opponent's pawn is captured and returns to Charkoni — it must re-enter with a Grace throw. Capturing earns an extra turn.
  6. Blocking. Two or more pawns of the same colour on a non-castle square form a block. Opponents cannot pass through. But they can be captured if the opponent lands on that exact square.
  7. Finishing. A pawn completes the journey by moving back up its home arm's centre column and landing exactly on Charkoni. No overshooting — if the throw is too high, the pawn cannot move.
  8. Team victory. The winning team is the one whose combined 8 pawns (across 2 players) all reach home first. A player who finishes all 4 of their own pawns can then use extra turns to assist their partner.
  9. Optional rules. In some regional versions: a player cannot begin the home journey unless they have captured at least one opponent. "Pyada" rule: once all 4 pawns are on the board, a Grace throw earns 1 bonus square.
06 Didactic Value — What the Game Teaches

Pachisi has survived 1,600+ years because it teaches something real every time it's played. This is the heart of Krida's mission — surfacing the wisdom embedded in these games. Here's what Pachisi teaches:

🎲
Probability & Risk
Cowrie throws are binomial distributions. Players learn intuitively that 3 is the most likely throw, 25 is rare and precious. Risk vs. reward: advance into danger, or shelter in a castle?
🤝
Team Cooperation
You must sacrifice your own progress for your partner's gain. Unlike Ludo, selfishness loses. Learning when to block, when to capture, and when to serve as a decoy — all cooperative instincts.
🏯
Strategic Patience
Knowing when to stay in a castle and wait vs. when to move is a core strategic tension. Premature advance gets you captured. Waiting too long lets the opponent build momentum.
🔢
Counting & Arithmetic
Children playing Pachisi unconsciously count squares, add throws, and plan several moves ahead. The board's 96-square track is a physical number line. The game teaches arithmetic without calling it arithmetic.
🌀
Circular Cosmology
The cross shape represents the four directions of the universe, with the Charkoni as the cosmic centre — Brahma's seat. Movement counter-clockwise mirrors pradakshina (ritual circumambulation) in Hindu practice.
👑
Royal Heritage
Akbar's court turned a game into governance theatre. Playing Pachisi connects players to 16th-century Mughal court life, the Mahabharata, and a 3,000-year chain of Indian ludic tradition.

Board Aesthetic — Embroidered Silk
The board background should feel like deep crimson silk — CSS texture or SVG hatching to simulate woven fabric. The grid lines are rendered as gold thread (thin, warm, slightly irregular). Castle squares are a different shade — dark burgundy or deep ochre — visually distinct. The Charkoni gets a lotus mandala treatment with animated petals.

Traditional Pachisi pawns are beehive-shaped (conical, wider at base, tapering to a rounded top). NOT Ludo flat discs — that's the key visual differentiator. Each pawn: player colour body, gold-trim top, subtle 3D shadow beneath. On hover: gentle glow in player colour. Captured pawns: small animation of spinning and shrinking as they return home.
🐚
Cowrie Shell Throw — The Showpiece Animation
This is the unforgettable moment. When a player throws, 6 cowrie shells are scattered across a small canvas — they tumble, rotate, and settle. Some land aperture-up (white inner shown), some land aperture-down (curved shell shown). The count animates as each shell settles. The result flashes with the throw value name in Devanagari: "पचीस!" A tabla beat plays. No plain dice roller could compete with this.
🏛️
UI Frame — Mughal Architecture
The game screen border should evoke Mughal jaali (lattice) screens — subtle geometric repeat pattern around the edges. Score panels use arched headers (like Mughal archway shapes). Typography: Cinzel or similar serif for all headings and Sanskrit names. Devanagari labels appear alongside English everywhere.
✨
Microinteractions

Grace throw: brief golden burst on the throw panel. Valid move squares: gentle ivory highlight (not a harsh colour). Winning moment: the Charkoni lotus blooms and petals animate outward.
08 Design for Engagement & Retention

What separates a game people play once from one they revisit? Emotional moments, progressive disclosure, and a sense that the experience is richer each time they return. Here are the specific engagement levers for Pachisi on Krida:

Hook · First 10 Seconds
The Akbar Opening
Before the game loads, a brief animated historical vignette — text appears describing Akbar's life-size Pachisi court at Fatehpur Sikri. 4 seconds. Gives players immediate cultural context and makes the board feel like a royal invitation, not a puzzle.
Hook · Throw Ritual
The Cowrie Throw Ceremony
Clicking "Throw" isn't just a button — it triggers a full cowrie shell scatter animation. The sound of shells on a silk cloth, the settling, the reveal. This single moment is more memorable than any leaderboard. Players will throw just to watch it happen.
Engagement · Modes
Three Ways to Play
Solo: 1 player vs 3 AI bots (3 difficulty levels — Novice, Courtier, Akbar). Pass & Play: 2–4 players on same device, passing at each turn. Tutorial: Guided first game with historical tooltips explaining each rule.
Engagement · Discovery
Historical Fact Reveals
Each time a Grace throw happens, a small historical fact panel slides in: "Did you know — Pachisi is mentioned in the Mahabharata as 'Pasha'. The game Yudhishthira wagered everything on." Small, dismissible, delightful. Makes the game educational without feeling like school.
Retention · Progression
Regional Board Skins
Three visual board variants unlock as players explore: Fatehpur Sikri (deep red, Mughal arches), Ellora Caves (stone-carved look, ochre palette), Rajasthan Silk (rich magenta and gold embroidery). Same rules, wildly different atmosphere.
Retention · Shareability
Capture Moments
When you capture an opponent, a brief "shareable" animation plays with the player's colour and a dramatic quote. "The Emperor's pawn has fallen!" — something theatrical. Designed to be screenshot-worthy and shareable. Makes every capture feel consequential.
Revisit · Hub Loop
Back to Krida Hub
At game end, a natural bridge back to the Krida hub: "You've mastered the game of kings. Explore another ancient Indian game →" with previews of Moksha Pattam, Bagh Chal, and Pallankuzhi. The hub becomes a world to explore, not just a collection.
Depth · Strategy Aid
Move Hints (Togglable)
New players can enable "Courtier's Advice" — subtle highlighting of the statistically best move (advance the furthest pawn, seek a capture, shelter in castle). Not forced — a toggleable aid that helps beginners ramp up without frustrating them.
09 Key Animation Specifications
🐚 Cowrie Shell Throw
6 shell SVGs scatter from a central point using randomised translate + rotate transforms. Each settles after 400–600ms with a slight bounce (cubic-bezier). Aperture-up vs aperture-down determined by random + game logic. Counter increments as each shell settles. Total animation: ~800ms. Sound: cloth rustling sfx.
♟️ Pawn Movement
Pawn slides square by square along the path (not a direct jump to destination). Each square hop: 80ms ease-in-out. This makes counting visible and satisfying. If move is 10 squares, the whole animation takes ~800ms. Castle arrival: brief gold pulse. Home arrival: pawn glows and slightly enlarges.
💥 Capture
Captured pawn does a 360° rotation + scale-down to 0 over 400ms, then reappears at Charkoni with a scale-up from 0 + slight bounce. Capturing pawn briefly expands (scale 1.3 → 1) as it lands. A brief flash in the attacking player's colour on the capturing square.
🌸 Charkoni Win Bloom
When the final pawn returns home, the Charkoni lotus petals animate outward and scale up. Gold particles radiate from the centre. The winning team's colour floods the Charkoni background briefly. A Sanskrit victory phrase appears: "विजय!" (Victory!). Duration: 2 seconds, then game summary slides in.
✦ Grace Throw Celebration
When shells show a Grace value (6, 10, or 25), a brief golden burst radiates from the throw area. The value name in Devanagari appears large and fades: "पचीस!" The "throw again" prompt pulses invitingly. Duration: 600ms, non-blocking.
10 Technical Approach

🗺️
Board as a Path Array
The board is a pre-computed array of 96 squares per player (the outer track) + 8 home column squares, each with (x,y) screen coordinates and metadata (isCastle, isHomeColumn, isCharkoni, ownerColor). Pawn position = index in this array. Movement = increment index. This makes the entire game engine clean and fast.
🤖
AI Strategy (Heuristic)
The AI evaluates each possible move by scoring: capture opportunity (+50), advance toward home (+distance), castle safety (+10 if threatened), avoid vulnerability (-20 if would be exposed). Simple but effective. Three levels adjust how often the AI picks the optimal vs a random move. No minimax needed — Pachisi's branching factor is low enough for greedy heuristics.
📱
Mobile Considerations
The board should be responsive — SVG viewBox scales cleanly. Pawns need touch targets of at least 44×44px. The cowrie throw should be triggerable by tapping the throw area, with a gentle swipe gesture as an optional throw ritual. Landscape mode on mobile shows board left, controls right.
11 Build Plan — Step 3 Sequence
  1. Board SVG Construction. Draw the cruciform board with all 4 arms, Charkoni at centre, 12 castle squares marked, grid lines rendered as gold thread. Add lotus mandala to Charkoni. Add player colour zones.
  2. Path Data Layer. Pre-compute the 96-square outer track + 8-square home column for all 4 players as JSON arrays with (x,y) coordinates. This is the invisible foundation the entire game runs on.
  3. Cowrie Shell Throw Mechanic. Build the shell scatter animation, shell SVGs (aperture-up / aperture-down states), probability engine, and throw value display with Devanagari name.
  4. Pawn Rendering & Movement. Render beehive-shaped SVG pawns in 4 colours. Animate step-by-step movement along the path array. Handle stacking (multiple pawns on same square).
  5. Core Game Logic. Implement: Grace throws / board entry, capture detection, castle immunity, home column logic, exact Charkoni landing, team turn management.
  6. UI Shell. Player turn indicator, score panel (pawns home count), history log ("Red captured Yellow's pawn!"), Devanagari + English labels throughout.
  7. AI Opponents. Implement heuristic AI for 1–3 bot players. Three difficulty levels. Add thinking delay (500ms) so moves don't feel instant.
  8. Win Detection & Celebration. Detect team win, trigger Charkoni bloom animation, show victory panel with game summary.
  9. Polish. Sound effects, castle shimmer effects, historical fact tooltips on Grace throws, intro vignette animation, mobile touch optimisation.
