பல்லாங்குழி · Tamil Nadu / Sri Lanka · Ancient

Two rows of seven cups. Sow seeds clockwise, one per cup. Land on four to capture. India's only living mancala tradition — played at harvest festivals for millennia, referenced in 6th-century Tamil epics, and deeply tied to the rhythms of agricultural life.

CULTURAL HISTORY

Carved in stone.
Played for millennia.

These games are not museum pieces. They were played on temple floors, under banyan trees, at harvest festivals. They still are.

NAVAKANKARI · PRE-1000 CE

Carved into Angkor Wat

Identical Navakankari boards are scratched into the stone floors of Angkor Wat in Cambodia, Mehragarh Fort in Rajasthan, and the ancient ghats of Varanasi — proof that the game travelled with merchants, soldiers, and pilgrims across Asia.

"The most strategic two-player game of the ancient world. Its opening theory rivals early chess."
PALLANKUZHI · SANGAM ERA (~300 BCE)

A Women's Harvest Game

Referenced in the Manimekalai (6th century CE) and Sangam poetry, Pallankuzhi was traditionally played by women during Pongal, the Tamil harvest festival. The act of sowing seeds in the game mirrors the ritual of sowing paddy in fields.

"The counting and sowing mechanic is deeply satisfying — a meditation on rhythm and harvest."
GLOBAL PARALLEL

Before Chess

Navakankari predates Chaturanga (proto-chess) on the Indian subcontinent. Its Western cousin, Nine Men's Morris, appears in ancient Egypt (1400 BCE), was played in the Roman Empire, and was mentioned by Shakespeare. The Indian form is the most strategically deep variant known.

THE KRIDA MISSION

Preservation Through Play

Both games face extinction — displaced by digital entertainment and the loss of the cultural contexts that transmitted them. Krida rebuilds them as living, playable experiences: faithful to their mechanics, beautiful to behold, available to anyone with a browser.
